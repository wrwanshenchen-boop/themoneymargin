# Should Working Adults Buy Insurance? My Real Trade-Off Process

On an overcast Thursday morning in October 2024, I was sitting at my kitchen counter in Austin with my laptop open, staring at our company's annual benefits portal.

Open enrollment closed at 5:00 p.m. that day.

On the screen was an optional voluntary benefits menu: critical illness insurance, supplemental hospital indemnity, pet insurance, vision plus, and voluntary term life insurance.

Each item had a small blue checkbox next to a deduction number:  
- Supplemental life: $8.40/paycheck  
- Short-term disability buy-up: $14.20 per bi-weekly paycheck  
- Pet insurance: $28.50/paycheck  
- Hospital indemnity: $11.10/paycheck  

If I checked every box, my bi-weekly paycheck would shrink by roughly **$62**, or about **$135 every single month**.

My cat was asleep on the counter next to my coffee mug, completely oblivious.  
I had about $5,000 sitting in my High-Yield Savings Account at that point, my credit cards were paid off, and I make $72,000 a year as a Product Operations Specialist.

I remember thinking, *Am I being irresponsible if I uncheck these boxes, or am I just buying expensive peace of mind that I don't actually need?*

For young working adults in their late twenties, insurance feels like a scam when you're healthy, and an emergency you can't afford when you're sick.

It took me three open-enrollment cycles and one expensive out-of-pocket dental bill to realize the truth:

Insurance is never an investment. It is not something you buy to "get your money's worth."  
Insurance is simply paying a known, controlled fee today to eliminate a catastrophic, life-altering risk tomorrow—so you can sleep through the night.

## The 4 Questions I Ask Before Buying Any Policy

Insurance salespeople and HR portals love to frame every policy as "essential."  
If you buy every policy offered to you, you will protect yourself against every theoretical disaster while guaranteeing you have zero cash left to build an emergency fund.

To cut through the noise, I created four strict filters I run before signing any policy document:

1. **Could this specific event wipe out my entire savings or bankrupt me?**  
   If an unexpected event costs $400 (like a broken smartphone screen), an insurance policy is a waste of money. If an event could cost $85,000 (like major surgery or a multi-car collision on I-35), insurance is mandatory.
2. **Does anyone depend on my income to survive right now?**  
   If I have no children and my partner earns her own income, buying a $1,000,000 life insurance policy is subsidizing an insurance company's profit margin.
3. **What is my maximum out-of-pocket exposure versus my liquid cash reserves?**  
   If my health insurance has an Out-of-Pocket Maximum of $4,500, and I have $6,000 in my savings account, I can absorb the worst-case medical deductible without needing expensive supplemental riders.
4. **Is this an insurance product, or an overpriced savings account disguised as insurance?**  
   Whole life, universal life, and retail gadget warranties are notoriously inefficient products designed to generate massive agent commissions.

## The Mistake That Cost Me $680: The Low-Deductible Trap

Back in 2021, when my finances were chaotic and my savings account had $300 in it, I did what many fearful people do: I picked the most expensive, lowest-deductible health and auto insurance plans available.

I paid an extra $160 every month out of my paycheck to have a $500 health deductible instead of a $2,500 deductible.

I thought I was being cautious.

Here is what actually happened:  
I went to the doctor exactly twice that entire year for routine checkups that were 100% covered under preventative care rules.

I paid **$1,920 in extra premiums** to protect myself against a deductible risk that never materialized.

When you have no cash savings, you are forced to pay high monthly premiums for low deductibles because you cannot afford a $1,000 surprise bill.  
Once I built my $1,500 Tier 1 emergency fund in 2023, I immediately flipped the equation:
- Raised auto deductible from $500 to $1,000
- Switched to an HDHP with a higher deductible
- Pocketed the premium difference ($80/month) and routed it directly into an HSA

A cash emergency fund is the best insurance policy in the world: it charges zero premiums, pays you interest, and covers every category of life without an exclusions clause.

## The Exact Script I Used to Audit My Auto Policy

In March 2024, auto insurance rates spiked across Texas. My renewal notice showed a $38/month increase, bringing my premium to $206.

Instead of accepting the rate hike, I called my insurer directly and used this script:

> *"Hi, this is Daniel Reyes. I’ve been an policyholder on account [Policy Number] for over three years with a clean driving record. I received my renewal notice with an unexpected premium increase. I’d like to review my current coverages line-by-line to adjust my deductibles and verify that all available discounts—such as low annual commuter mileage, paperless billing, and defensive driving credits—are fully applied before I look at competing quotes."*

The representative reviewed my profile:
- Increased my collision deductible from $500 to $1,000 (I knew my emergency fund covered this)
- Updated my annual estimated commuting mileage from 12,000 miles down to 7,500 miles because I work hybrid
- Applied a paperless autopay credit

The call took 14 minutes.  
After the call, it dropped to $135—nearly $850 a year less than the proposed renewal, and $33 less than what I was paying before.

## If You Want to Try This Tomorrow

1. **Pull your current insurance deductibles:** Open your auto, renter’s, and health insurance apps. Write down your deductible for each on a single index card.
2. **Compare your deductibles to your emergency savings:** If your auto deductible is $500, but you have $2,000 in liquid savings, consider raising your deductible to $1,000 to immediately lower your monthly premium.
3. **Check your credit card perks for hidden coverage:** Log into your primary credit card portal and read the "Guide to Benefits." See if it includes built-in cell phone protection, auto rental collision damage waivers, or purchase protection.
4. **Audit your open-enrollment menu:** If you have no legal dependents or mortgage debt, decline expensive supplemental life and accident riders. Keep the employer-paid base policy.
5. **Call your auto insurer before your next renewal:** Use the script above to verify your actual annual mileage and ask for an account audit.

## Clear Takeaway

Insurance is not about preparing for every conceivable inconvenience. It is about ring-fencing catastrophic financial ruin.

When you are living paycheck to paycheck, you overpay for insurance because you are terrified of a $500 surprise. Once you build a modest cash cushion, you realize you can self-insure the small annoyances and pay only for the walls that protect against real disasters.

I still pay my premiums every month, but I don't treat them like lottery tickets hoping for a payout. I pay them for the same reason I lock my apartment door when I leave for work: not because I expect a break-in today, but because it frees up my mind to think about everything else.
